**When does EUDR apply?** As checked on **5 November 2026**, the main application date is **30 December 2026** for large and medium businesses, with **30 June 2027** for most micro and small businesses. Certain products newly added to the rules have a separate **30 December 2027** start date. For Nigerian suppliers, the practical question is when your product and your EU buyer's activity come within scope.

The EU Deforestation Regulation is already in force, but its main business obligations have not yet started applying. Buyer preparation can nevertheless affect your next order. My advice is to use the remaining time to establish where your goods came from and whether your buyer can use your evidence.

## The current EUDR deadlines

The [European Commission's current implementation timetable](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/forests/deforestation/eu-deforestation-regulation-eudr_en) distinguishes these dates:

- **30 December 2026:** large and medium businesses. This date also applies to micro and small operators already covered by the EU Timber Regulation.
- **30 June 2027:** most other micro and small businesses. The business must qualify for that treatment; “SME” is not a blanket six-month extension, because medium-sized businesses start earlier.
- **30 December 2027:** products newly brought into scope by Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/2102. This is a product-specific transition, not another general postponement.

Do not assume that a small Nigerian farm gives a large EU importer until June 2027. Establish the size and role of the business carrying the EU obligation, the exact product, and the applicable transition. The [Commission's roles and responsibilities guide](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/nature-and-biodiversity/deforestation-regulation-implementation/roles-and-responsibilities_en) explains those distinctions.

For contracts spanning a deadline, ask the importer and customs adviser to confirm the treatment of the consignment before loading. A shipping date on its own is not a safe basis for assuming an exemption.

## Which agricultural trade is affected?

The regulation covers **cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soya, and wood**, together with specified derived products. Coverage depends on the product codes in **Annex I**, rather than every item made from a commodity or every agricultural export. Start by agreeing the correct customs classification with the buyer.

For example, cocoa exporters need to examine the cocoa product they supply; a business selling a different crop should not assume the same EUDR obligations automatically apply. Check the current Annex and any product-specific start date through the [Commission's product-scope resources](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/nature-and-biodiversity/deforestation-regulation-implementation_en).

## What EU buyers must be able to establish

In-scope products must be **deforestation-free**, **legally produced**, and covered by the required statement or declaration. The deforestation cut-off remains **31 December 2020**; harvested wood also has forest-degradation requirements. An application-date extension does not move that environmental cut-off. See the [Commission's overview of the requirements](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/forests/deforestation/eu-deforestation-regulation-eudr_en).

The upstream operator must collect evidence, assess risk and, where necessary, reduce that risk before market placement. Under the ordinary due-diligence route, missing required information means the product cannot be placed on the EU market. Import declarations need the relevant statement reference or declaration identifier. This is more than asking a supplier to sign a letter. The [Commission's due-diligence guide](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/nature-and-biodiversity/deforestation-regulation-implementation/understand-due-diligence_en) sets out the process.

## Who files: the Nigerian supplier or the EU buyer?

A Nigerian producer supplying an EU importer does not automatically become the business responsible for filing in the EU system. The importer is generally the upstream operator. However, a non-EU business that itself places goods on the EU market can have operator obligations too. Agree the actual roles rather than relying on where each company is registered.

The revised rules also distinguish upstream operators from downstream businesses; every participant does not submit a fresh due-diligence statement. See the [current explanation of supply-chain roles](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/nature-and-biodiversity/deforestation-regulation-implementation/roles-and-responsibilities_en).

For the Nigerian exporter, the work is still commercially important: provide the supplier, plot, production and legality information your importer needs. Establish who gathers it, who checks it, who files, and who resolves inconsistencies before the cargo moves.

## Nigeria's risk rating matters

Nigeria is currently listed as **standard risk** in the [Commission's country classification list](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/countries-and-partnerships/country-classification-list_en). This is not a ban on Nigerian commodities and does not determine whether a particular farm is compliant.

It does mean an operator sourcing Nigerian products cannot rely on the simplified due-diligence treatment reserved for qualifying low-risk sourcing. Low-risk sourcing still requires evidence; routing goods through a low-risk country does not change where they were grown or remove mixing concerns. See the [Commission's explanation of country benchmarking](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/countries-and-partnerships_en).

## A practical checklist before the next EU order

1. **Confirm scope and responsibility.** Record the product code, application date and business responsible for the EU filing. Get the buyer's data requirements in writing.
2. **Connect the lot to its suppliers and plots.** Give farmers and plots stable identifiers, and connect purchase and aggregation records to the shipment. Keep unverified material out of an EU-designated lot.
3. **Check the mapping format.** For the ordinary geolocation route, non-cattle plots larger than four hectares need polygons; a single point is not enough. Coordinates need at least six decimal places. Validate the files before sending them. The [official Information System documentation](https://eudr.webcloud.ec.europa.eu/tracesnt/help/eudr-documentation/operator/api/due-diligence-statement-v4.html) describes the data requirements.
4. **Assemble a usable evidence file.** Match product descriptions, quantities, production dates, supplier details, plot data, deforestation checks and relevant legality documents. Explain discrepancies instead of sending an unexplained folder of files.
5. **Close the buyer's questions before dispatch.** Test one complete lot with the importer, resolve missing evidence, and agree how the filing reference reaches the customs declarant. Build time for corrections into the shipment plan.

These are preparation steps, not a substitute for the operator's risk assessment. Documents relating to the operator's due diligence must be retained for **five years**. Agree record access and retention with your buyer. The [official due-diligence guidance](https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/nature-and-biodiversity/deforestation-regulation-implementation/understand-due-diligence_en) explains the evidence and record-keeping duties.

## How EUDR changes the commercial deal

**Market access:** once the relevant obligations apply, a buyer cannot solve missing compliance evidence simply by accepting a lower price. The transaction needs a compliant route onto the EU market.

**Supplier selection:** my practical assessment is that buyers will have a stronger reason to favour suppliers whose records they can check. An unmapped supplier network can therefore become an order-winning problem even where the farms themselves meet the environmental requirement.

**Costs and cash flow:** mapping, verification, record management and resolving discrepancies take work. Agree who pays for that work. If a data problem delays a shipment or acceptance, storage costs and later payment can erode the margin. These are commercial risks to plan for, not a prediction that every shipment will be delayed.

**Contracts:** spell out evidence delivery dates, responsibilities for errors, treatment of rejected lots and payment conditions. A buyer's preparation deadline may be earlier than the legal application date; label the two clearly.

**Other markets:** EUDR is an EU market rule, not a worldwide export licence. Alternative destinations have their own requirements. Better traceability can support buyer confidence, but does not automatically establish compliance elsewhere.

## What I would do now

With the main December deadline approaching, start with one real order. Identify the importer, confirm the product code, trace the lot back to its plots, and ask the buyer to test the evidence. Then repeat a working process across the supplier network.

That is a more useful starting point than collecting farm coordinates without connecting them to the goods you intend to sell. For the farmer-data side, read [smallholder digitization — what actually works](/blog/smallholder-digitization-what-works).

**Source check:** deadlines, scope and implementation guidance reviewed on **5 November 2026** against the European Commission resources linked above. Recheck those sources before agreeing a shipment that crosses an application date.
