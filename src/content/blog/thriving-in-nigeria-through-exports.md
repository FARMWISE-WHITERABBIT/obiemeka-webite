**How can you build an export business from Nigeria?** Start with a service or product you can deliver reliably, find the overseas customer who needs it, and work out the costs and risks before committing money. The three paths discussed here are digital services, agricultural commodities, and minerals. None guarantees a profit.

During a walk through the community where I run my plantation in Enugu, I recorded a video about a question many Nigerians are asking: how do you build a decent living when everyday costs keep rising and your income struggles to keep up?

My argument was simple. Look beyond the local market. Think about what you can offer to customers outside Nigeria, whether that is a skill, an agricultural commodity, or a mineral product.

That is an opportunity, not a promise of easy money. Getting paid in foreign currency does not remove the work, costs, or risk of building a business. But it gives you a wider market to serve.

## First, export a skill

Software development is one example, but the bigger idea is a service you can deliver digitally to a customer abroad. You can live in Nigeria while doing useful work for people elsewhere.

The sequence matters. Learn the skill, practise it, and become good enough that someone will pay for the result. Then learn how to position yourself and find clients. Taking a course and attracting paying customers are different tasks; you need to do both.

I was involved in exporting services before moving into commodities. That experience is part of why I see exports as a useful way to think about earning: start with what you can deliver well, then find the market that values it.

## Second, export agricultural commodities

Cocoa, shea butter, ginger, and soybeans are among the products I mentioned in the video. This is the path I now work in, and it is the one I can speak about most directly.

A product being in demand does not automatically make your business profitable. You still have to get production or sourcing right, meet the buyer's requirements, and deliver what you agreed to supply. The opportunity sits inside that operation.

There is also a difference between growing a crop and trading it. Before committing money, be clear about which part of the chain you intend to handle and what you need to learn to do it reliably.

## Third, consider the mineral export path carefully

The third route I mentioned was minerals, including rare earth minerals. It is a separate business from agricultural exports and needs its own knowledge, lawful sourcing, and understanding of buyer requirements.

My practical experience in this discussion is with services and agricultural commodities. Mentioning minerals as an export category is not a recommendation to enter it without understanding the sector.

The common thread across all three paths is access to a wider customer base. The practical requirements are different.

## What our ginger work illustrates

In the video, I spoke about preparing ginger for dry-season production. The commercial thinking was to aim for a selling window when supply is tighter, rather than simply follow the same production timing as everyone else.

That can create an opportunity for a better price. It does not guarantee a better profit. Your production costs and actual harvest still have to make the numbers work.

Ginger is also a useful reminder that an attractive market cannot rescue poor execution. Weak planting material can affect production. Mistakes during the crop cycle can become expensive. Before thinking about the eventual sale, you need to understand what it takes to produce a crop worth selling.

## Start with the operation you can understand

Setting up our plantation in Enugu has taught me how expensive starting from scratch can be. Time spent learning from people already doing the work can help you recognise mistakes before you pay for them yourself.

If you are deciding where to begin, choose one path and get specific. What will you sell? Who needs it? What will it cost you to deliver? What could go wrong before you get paid?

You do not need to pursue all three routes. You need one that fits your skills, resources, and ability to execute. Looking beyond Nigeria's local market opens up possibilities; building the capability to serve that market is what makes the opportunity useful.
