Diaspora investors and family offices ask me some version of the same question: is agricultural land in Nigeria actually investable, or is this a story that only works in a pitch deck? The honest answer is that it's investable, but it behaves nothing like the asset classes most investors are used to comparing it against.

## It's not farmland, and it's not private equity

Traditional farmland investment assumes you're buying land and someone else operates it. Traditional agri private equity assumes you're buying into a going operating business. Agro real estate development sits between the two — you're buying a plot, but the returns depend entirely on whether the development, harvest, and export operation running on top of that plot is actually competent. The land is the collateral; the operation is the return driver.

## The horizon is longer than most pitches admit

Cocoa, the crop I know best, doesn't produce a meaningful yield until year three or four. Anyone promising returns inside 18 months is either describing a different crop entirely or not being straight with you about the maturity curve. A 5–7 year horizon isn't a marketing choice — it's biology.

## The questions that actually separate good deals from bad ones

I tell every investor I advise to ask the same three things before committing capital to any agro real estate deal, mine included:

- Who operates the land day to day, and what's their track record on a comparable crop?
- What's the exit — a buyer at harvest, an export contract, or a hope?
- What happens to your capital if the operator disappears? Is the land titled in a way that protects you?

## Where the conflict-of-interest question comes in

I run WhiteRabbit Agro, which does exactly this kind of development. When I advise an investor and WhiteRabbit isn't the right fit for what they're trying to do, I say so in writing. That's not generosity — it's the only way the advisory has any credibility at all. An investor who can't trust the recommendation isn't getting advice, they're getting a sales pitch with extra steps.

## What good operator discipline actually looks like

The plots that perform are the ones where someone treats the plantation like a business with a P&L, not a hobby with a harvest date. Cost tracking per hectare, realistic yield forecasting instead of best-case projections, and a written plan for what happens if a harvest underperforms — these are boring things, and they're the entire difference between a plantation investment that returns capital and one that quietly erodes it over five years.
